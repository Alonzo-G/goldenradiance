## Why the wear simulation comes first

Most suppliers test the base metal. EN 12472 requires simulating two years of
wear first, then testing what actually touches skin. That is where thin plating
fails: a coating that reads clean out of the bath can release nickel once the
wear layer is gone.

## The limits, as the standards state them

- **Coated articles:** 0.5 µg/cm²/week after EN 12472 wear simulation.
- **Pierced articles — ear posts and similar:** 0.2 µg/cm²/week, no exceptions.
  The stricter limit exists because a fresh piercing is an open wound in direct
  contact with the post.

Both numbers are properties of the standard, not of any specific product. A
product page that prints a limit next to a style without a report is asking you
to assume the result — which is exactly what the compliance center exists to
prevent.

## The three questions that separate a real report from a bought one

1. **Which standard and which method?** EN 1811 for uncoated, EN 1811 plus EN
   12472 wear simulation for coated. A report citing only EN 1811 for a plated
   style does not cover the article as sold.
2. **Which laboratory?** Cross-check the report number with the issuing
   laboratory directly. A report without all four attributes — standard,
   laboratory, report number, date — is not evidence.
3. **Which batch?** Plating baths drift. A report from six lots ago says nothing
   about the lot you are buying. Per-batch testing is the only version of this
   promise that can be kept in writing.

## Common failure patterns

Plating below the stated thickness, unverified base alloy, and reports issued
without wear simulation. All three are invisible in a product photo and obvious
in a spec sheet — which is why the spec sheet travels with every quotation.

## How to require this in your RFQ

State the destination market and any customer-specific limits in the RFQ notes.
The market selection in the RFQ form determines which standards apply, and the
report for the matching batch is attached to your quotation.
